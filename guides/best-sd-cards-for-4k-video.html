The Best SD Card for 4K Video (2026 Guide)

The fastest way to ruin a 4K shoot is a slow card: dropped frames, recording that stops mid-take, footage that won't transfer. This guide cuts through the alphabet soup of V30, V60, V90, and UHS ratings so you buy exactly the card your camera needs — no more, no less. The only spec that matters: the "V" number

SD cards print a wall of numbers — 200MB/s! 4K! U3! — but only one of them is a guarantee: the Video Speed Class, the "V" rating. It's the minimum sustained write speed the card must maintain, in megabytes per second. Sustained is the key word: a camera recording 4K never stops writing, so a card that spikes fast then slows down will drop your recording.

V ratingMin. sustained write= Mbps capacityBest for
V3030 MB/s240 MbpsStandard 4K (100–200 Mbps): 4K 24/30p, vlogs, YouTube, drones
V6060 MB/s480 MbpsHigh-bitrate 4K: 4K 60p, 10-bit codecs, higher frame rates
V9090 MB/s720 Mbps4K ALL-I above 400 Mbps, 6K/8K, professional cinema workflows

The 10-second rule: find your camera's highest video bitrate (in Mbps), divide by 8 — that's the minimum MB/s your card must sustain. If your camera records at 200 Mbps, you need at least 25 MB/s sustained → a V30 card. Give yourself one class of headroom if you can afford it; don't buy V90 for a 100 Mbps camera.

Ignore the big "170MB/s" / "300MB/s" numbers on the box — those are peak read speeds (for copying files off the card), not sustained write speeds. They tell you nothing about recording.

Before you buy: 4 things nobody prints on the box

  1. Buy from real retailers. Counterfeit cards are rampant on marketplaces — they report fake capacity and eat your footage. B&H, Adorama, Best Buy in-store, or the manufacturer direct. If you buy on Amazon, run H2testw/FakeFlashTest on the card before trusting it.
  2. UHS-I vs UHS-II slots matter. V60/V90 cards are UHS-II (two rows of contacts) and only hit their rated speeds in UHS-II slots. In a UHS-I slot they still work, but slower — check your camera's slot type before paying the UHS-II premium.
  3. Two cards beat one big one. One 512 GB card is a single point of failure. Two 256 GB cards cost about the same and halve your worst-case loss.
  4. SD vs microSD. MicroSD with an adapter works in a pinch but adds a failure point; for mission-critical recording, buy native full-size SD.

FAQ

Is V30 enough for 4K?

For standard 4K at 100–200 Mbps — yes. V30 guarantees 30 MB/s (240 Mbps) sustained. It only falls short for 4K 60p/120p high-bitrate modes and ALL-I codecs above ~400 Mbps.

Is a more expensive card safer?

Not automatically. A genuine V30 from SanDisk, Lexar, Kingston, Sony, Angelbird, or ProGrade is safer than a counterfeit V90. Buy genuine from a reputable retailer first; buy speed second.

Can I use one card for photos and video?

Yes — the V-rating governs video; stills bursts care more about the camera's buffer and the card's write speed. A V60 UHS-II card is a good dual-purpose card for hybrid shooters.

How long do SD cards last?

NAND wears with writes, but a quality card typically survives years of normal use. Replace cards that develop errors, and never reuse a card that failed mid-shoot — retire it.
